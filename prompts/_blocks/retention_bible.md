How a short story holds someone who is one thumb-flick from leaving. These are craft
rules about the story itself. They are NOT permission to bait, mislead or manipulate -
the title must be true of the video, the hook must be paid off, and anything the opening
promises the ending has to deliver. A story that wins the first five seconds and lies
about the next five is worse than one nobody clicked.

THE FIRST LINE IS THE WHOLE JOB.
- Open in the middle of trouble. The first spoken line must be someone reacting to
  something that has ALREADY gone wrong, not setting up a world in which something might.
- No establishing, no throat-clearing, no "once upon a time", no character introducing
  themselves, no narrator explaining the setting. The audience works out who these people
  are from what they are doing, exactly as they do in life.
- Name the trouble in concrete, specific terms in the first sentence: a missing thing, a
  broken thing, an accusation, a deadline, a debt, a door that will not open. "Something
  strange happened" is not a hook; "the shop's takings are forty rupees short and only
  two people had the key" is.

OPEN A QUESTION AND KEEP ONE OPEN.
- The first thirty seconds must plant a question the viewer wants answered - who did it,
  what is behind that, will they make it, why is this one lying.
- Answer it in a way that opens the next one. There should be no point in the story where
  every question the viewer has is already answered; that is the moment they leave.
- Close the last one properly. An unpaid setup is not a cliffhanger, it is a cheat.

ESCALATE, DO NOT REPEAT.
- Every scene must change the situation. If a scene could be cut without breaking the
  next one, it is a scene about nothing - cut it and spend the seconds elsewhere.
- Raise the cost each beat. What they stand to lose at the climax must be visibly worse
  than what they stood to lose at the start.
- At least one real TURN: something the viewer believed turns out to be wrong. Plant its
  evidence honestly and early, so the turn is a surprise on first watch and obvious on
  second. A twist with nothing planted is a cheat and reads as one.

WRITE FOR A SMALL SCREEN WITH THE SOUND ON.
- Two speaking characters in a scene, three at the very most. A viewer cannot track four
  voices on a phone.
- Short lines. People interrupt, contradict and change the subject; they do not deliver
  paragraphs. A line over about twenty-five words should be split or cut.
- The emotion has to be legible on a face: wanting, fearing, lying, deciding. A feeling
  that can only be narrated cannot be animated.
- Every line must do a job - advance the plot, reveal character, or land a joke. A line
  doing none of the three comes out.

END SO THEY WOULD WATCH ANOTHER.
- The last beat lands the emotional payoff, not a summary and not a moral.
- The final line should be the best line: a decision, a reversal, or a joke that pays off
  something planted in the opening. The viewer should feel the shape close.
